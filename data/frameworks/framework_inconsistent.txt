# Consequentialist Moral System

**Preamble.** The moral goodness of an action is determined by the goodness of its consequences. Furthermore, every action must also respect the rights of all other agents. This system provides all of the criteria that a morally good action must satisfy. All other principles and considerations, whether practical or speculative, whether coming from authority or from historical practice, are completely irrelevant with regard to the moral goodness of an act.

1. **Consequence-dependence.** The moral value of an action depends entirely on its consequences, and not on any feature of the action itself, such as duties, intentions, or circumstances.

2. **Permissibility.** An action is morally permitted if and only if its expected net good is at least as great as that of every alternative available to the agent. When alternatives tie, each of these is permitted. An action is never permitted if it does not maximize the net good.

3. **Goods.** Primary goods are the bodily, mental, and emotional health and well-being of all cognitive beings. Secondary goods are the objects, conditions, and institutions that directly or indirectly promote primary goods, and they have value only to the extent that they do so.

4. **Aggregation.** Net good is the sum of the goods and harms to every affected being, with each being's good counted equally.

5. **Uncertainty.** Where outcomes are uncertain, the expected net good of an action is the sum of the net good of each possible outcome, weighted by its probability as reasonably estimated from the evidence available to the agent.

6. **Respect for rights.** An action must never be chosen if it violates another agent's right or rights.

8. **Examples of rights.** These include the freedom to manage one's own life, body, and possessions without interference from others.

8. **Rules of thumb.** The preceding articles state the criterion of right action, not a decision procedure. Agents may follow standards or rules that reliably produce actions meeting this criterion. Such rules are justified only by their reliability. An agent who follows a well-justified rule in good faith is not blameworthy when it yields a suboptimal result in a particular case.
